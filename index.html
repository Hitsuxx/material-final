Calculation

Stress, strain & elastic constants

The core bar problem — appears in every exam. Master the chain: force → area → stress → strain → modulus → Poisson → diameter change.

Key formulas
QuantityMathematical formNote
Cross-section area (circle)$$A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$$mm² when d in mm; never use d directly in σ
Normal stress$$\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$$MPa = N mm⁻²
Axial strain$$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L}$$Dimensionless
Young's modulus$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$MPa or GPa; 1 GPa = 1000 MPa
Poisson's ratio$$\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{lat}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}}$$Typically 0.25–0.35 for metals
Diameter change$$\Delta d = -\nu\,\frac{\Delta L}{L}\,d$$Negative in tension (bar thins)
E–G–ν relationship$$E = 2G(1+\nu) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nu = \frac{E}{2G}-1$$Rearrange to get ν when G is given
Shear stress$$\tau = \frac{F_s}{A_s}$$A_s = shear face area
Shear modulus$$G = \frac{\tau}{\gamma}$$γ = shear strain = x/L
Secant modulus$$E_\text{sec} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\text{chosen point}}$$Not the initial slope — take σ÷ε at that point
Elastic strain energy density$$U = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\varepsilon$$Area under the linear part of the σ–ε curve
Total work (trapezium rule)$$W = \sum_i \frac{F_i + F_{i+1}}{2}\,(e_{i+1}-e_i)$$1 kN·mm = 1 J
True stress / true strain$$\sigma_\text{true}=\sigma_\text{nom}(1+\varepsilon),\quad \varepsilon_\text{true}=\ln(1+\varepsilon)$$Accounts for actual reduced area

Worked examples
2024/25 · 2022/23 · 2021/22 — every year7 marks
A metal bar is 175 mm long, diameter 14 mm. A 20 kN tensile force causes an extension of 0.14 mm. The shear modulus $G = 65\,\text{GPa}$.

(i) Calculate the modulus of elasticity.
(ii) Calculate Poisson's ratio and the change in diameter.
Part (i) — modulus of elasticity
Step 1
Cross-sectional area: $$A = \frac{\pi (14)^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 196}{4} = 153.94\;\text{mm}^2$$
Step 2
Normal stress (convert force: 20 kN = 20 000 N): $$\sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{20\,000}{153.94} = \boxed{129.9\;\text{MPa}}$$
Step 3
Axial strain: $$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{0.14}{175} = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$$
Step 4
Young's modulus (linear elastic assumption): $$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{129.9}{8.0\times10^{-4}} = 162\,375\;\text{MPa} = \boxed{162.4\;\text{GPa}}$$
Part (ii) — Poisson's ratio and diameter change
Step 5
Use $E = 2G(1+\nu)$ rearranged: $$\nu = \frac{E}{2G} - 1 = \frac{162.4}{2\times65} - 1 = 1.249 - 1 = \boxed{0.249}$$ This is physically reasonable — metals typically lie between 0.25 and 0.35.
Step 6
The lateral strain is related to the axial strain by Poisson's ratio: $$\varepsilon_\text{lat} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_\text{axial} = -0.249 \times 8.0\times10^{-4} = -1.992\times10^{-4}$$ Diameter change: $$\Delta d = \varepsilon_\text{lat} \times d = -1.992\times10^{-4}\times14 = \boxed{-2.79\times10^{-3}\;\text{mm}}$$ The negative sign confirms the diameter decreases under tension — the Poisson contraction.

Creep & stress relaxation

Appears in every exam. Two numbers always required at each time: creep coefficient $\varphi$ and effective modulus $E_\text{eff}$.

Key formulas
QuantityMathematical formNote
Initial elastic strain$$\varepsilon_e = \frac{\sigma}{E}$$Calculate this first — always
Creep coefficient$$\varphi = \frac{\varepsilon_\text{creep}}{\varepsilon_e}$$Dimensionless; how many times the elastic strain the creep is
Total strain$$\varepsilon_\text{total} = \varepsilon_e + \varepsilon_\text{creep}$$Must add both components
Effective modulus$$E_\text{eff} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_\text{total}}$$Always less than $E$; uses total strain
Secondary creep rate law$$\frac{d\varepsilon}{dt} = C\,\sigma^n$$$C$ and $n$ are material constants
Finding $n$ from two tests$$n = \frac{\log\!\left(\dot\varepsilon_1/\dot\varepsilon_2\right)}{\log\!\left(\sigma_1/\sigma_2\right)}$$Divide the two rate equations to cancel $C$
Finding $C$$$C = \frac{\dot\varepsilon}{\sigma^n}$$Substitute known $n$ back into either equation
Time to reach target strain$$t = \frac{\varepsilon_\text{creep}}{\dot\varepsilon}$$Assumes constant secondary creep rate
Critical unit conversions — always convert before substituting:
1200 microstrain $= 1200\times10^{-6} = 0.0012$
$0.25\% = 0.25/100 = 0.0025$
$0.1\% = 0.001$

Worked example — creep coefficient and effective modulus (every exam year)
2024/25 · 2023/24 · 2021/22 — identical structure every year6 marks
A material with $E = 95\,\text{GPa}$ is loaded to $\sigma = 175\,\text{MPa}$. Creep strains measured at 3 days = 1200 microstrain and at 6 months = 0.25%.

Calculate the creep coefficient and effective modulus at each time.
Step 0 — elastic strain (same for both times)
$\varepsilon_e$
$$\varepsilon_e = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{175\;\text{MPa}}{95\,000\;\text{MPa}} = 1.842\times10^{-3}$$ This value is fixed throughout — stress is constant so the elastic component does not change with time.
At 3 days — creep strain = 1200 microstrain
Convert
$$\varepsilon_\text{creep} = 1200\times10^{-6} = 1.200\times10^{-3}$$ (A common error: entering 1200 directly — wrong by a factor of $10^6$)
$\varphi$
$$\varphi = \frac{\varepsilon_\text{creep}}{\varepsilon_e} = \frac{1.200\times10^{-3}}{1.842\times10^{-3}} = \boxed{0.651}$$ After 3 days, the creep strain is 65.1% of the initial elastic strain.
$\varepsilon_\text{tot}$
$$\varepsilon_\text{total} = 1.842\times10^{-3} + 1.200\times10^{-3} = 3.042\times10^{-3}$$
$E_\text{eff}$
$$E_\text{eff} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_\text{total}} = \frac{175}{3.042\times10^{-3}} = \boxed{57.5\;\text{GPa}}$$ The material behaves as if it has a much lower stiffness after 3 days of creep.
At 6 months — creep strain = 0.25%
Convert
$$\varepsilon_\text{creep} = \frac{0.25}{100} = 2.500\times10^{-3}$$
$\varphi$
$$\varphi = \frac{2.500\times10^{-3}}{1.842\times10^{-3}} = \boxed{1.357}$$ $\varphi > 1$ means the creep strain now exceeds the initial elastic strain.
$E_\text{eff}$
$$\varepsilon_\text{total} = 1.842\times10^{-3}+2.500\times10^{-3}=4.342\times10^{-3}$$ $$E_\text{eff} = \frac{175}{4.342\times10^{-3}} = \boxed{40.3\;\text{GPa}}$$ The effective stiffness has more than halved relative to the true $E = 95\;\text{GPa}$.

Fatigue & Miner's rule

Failure under repeated loading well below the ultimate strength. Master S–N curve reading and cumulative damage.

Key formulas
QuantityMathematical formNote
Stress range$$S = \sigma_\text{max} - \sigma_\text{min}$$If $\sigma_\text{min}$ is negative, $S$ increases
Mean stress$$\sigma_\text{mean} = \frac{\sigma_\text{max}+\sigma_\text{min}}{2}$$—
Miner's rule (damage sum)$$D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i} = 1 \quad\text{at failure}$$$n_i$ = cycles applied, $N_i$ = cycles to failure at $S_i$
Time to failure$$t = \frac{N}{f}$$$f$ = frequency in Hz
Using period$$t = N \cdot T$$$T$ = period of one cycle in seconds

Worked example — Miner's rule
Past paper type6 marks
A component experiences: $2\times10^5$ cycles at $S = 400\,\text{MPa}$ ($N = 5\times10^5$), then $1\times10^6$ cycles at $S = 200\,\text{MPa}$ ($N = 8\times10^6$). How many more cycles at $S = 300\,\text{MPa}$ ($N = 2\times10^6$) before failure?
Theory
Miner's rule assumes linear damage accumulation. Each block consumes fraction $n/N$ of the fatigue life. Failure when $\sum n_i/N_i = 1$.
Block 1
$$\frac{n_1}{N_1} = \frac{2\times10^5}{5\times10^5} = 0.400$$
Block 2
$$\frac{n_2}{N_2} = \frac{1\times10^6}{8\times10^6} = 0.125$$
Damage
$$D = 0.400 + 0.125 = 0.525 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{remaining} = 1 - 0.525 = 0.475$$
Block 3
$$n_3 = 0.475 \times N_3 = 0.475 \times 2\times10^6 = \boxed{9.5\times10^5\;\text{cycles}}$$

Variability & characteristic strength

Strength varies between samples. Structural design uses the characteristic strength — a statistically defined lower bound.

Key formulas
QuantityMathematical formNote
Mean strength$$\bar{\sigma} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i$$Sum all values, divide by count
Standard deviation$$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma_i-\bar{\sigma})^2}{n}}$$Measure of spread
Characteristic strength$$\sigma_k = \bar{\sigma} - k\,s$$$k$ depends on acceptable failure rate
Timber (5% failure)$$\sigma_k = \bar{\sigma} - 1.64\,s$$Standard for timber grading
Design stress$$\sigma_d = \frac{\sigma_k}{\gamma_m}$$$\gamma_m$: steel 1.15, concrete 1.6
Design load$$F_d = \gamma_f \cdot F_k$$$\gamma_f$: dead load 1.2, live 1.6
$k$ values to memorise:
10% failure → $k = 1.28$  |  5% (1 in 20) → $k = 1.64$  |  2% (1 in 50) → $k = 1.96$  |  1% → $k = 2.33$
Worked example — characteristic yield strength (2022/23 Q3c)
2022/23 Q3c4 marks
Ten mild steel samples give yield stresses (MPa): 265, 275, 271, 263, 255, 240, 263, 272, 270, 262. Calculate the characteristic yield strength at a 1 in 50 failure rate.
Step 1
1 in 50 = 2% failure rate → $k = 1.96$
Step 2
$$\bar\sigma = \frac{265+275+271+263+255+240+263+272+270+262}{10} = \frac{2636}{10} = \boxed{263.6\;\text{MPa}}$$
Step 3
Compute each $({\sigma_i - \bar\sigma})^2$: 1.96, 129.96, 54.76, 0.36, 73.96, 556.96, 0.36, 70.56, 40.96, 2.56. Sum = 932.4.
Step 4
$$s = \sqrt{\frac{932.4}{10}} = \sqrt{93.24} = \boxed{9.66\;\text{MPa}}$$
Step 5
$$\sigma_k = \bar\sigma - k\,s = 263.6 - 1.96\times9.66 = 263.6 - 18.93 = \boxed{244.7\;\text{MPa}}$$

Crystal density (FCC/BCC)

In every single exam. Always FCC. Always the same five-step method. Master this once and always score full marks.

Key formulas
QuantityMathematical formNote
Theoretical density$$\rho = \frac{n\,A}{N_A\,V_c}$$$n$ = atoms/cell, $A$ = atomic weight (g/mol), $N_A = 6.023\times10^{23}$
FCC atoms per cell$$n = 8\times\tfrac{1}{8}+6\times\tfrac{1}{2} = 4$$8 corner atoms + 6 face-centre atoms
BCC atoms per cell$$n = 8\times\tfrac{1}{8}+1 = 2$$8 corner + 1 body-centre
FCC lattice parameter$$a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r$$Atoms touch along face diagonal: $a\sqrt{2}=4r$
BCC lattice parameter$$a = \frac{4r}{\sqrt{3}}$$Atoms touch along body diagonal: $a\sqrt{3}=4r$
Unit cell volume$$V_c = a^3$$All cubic structures
Atomic packing factor$$\text{APF} = \frac{n\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3}{a^3}$$FCC = HCP = 0.74, BCC = 0.68, SC = 0.52
Critical unit conversion: $r$ given in nm → convert to cm before computing $V_c$.
Example: $r = 0.128\;\text{nm} = 0.128\times10^{-7}\;\text{cm} = 1.28\times10^{-8}\;\text{cm}$
Then $V_c = a^3$ is in cm³, and with $A$ in g/mol, $\rho$ comes out in g/cm³.
Worked example — copper (2024/25 Q4a — same every year, different metal)
2024/25 · 2023/24 · 2021/226 marks
Calculate the theoretical density of copper (FCC): $A = 63.5\,\text{g/mol}$, $r = 0.128\,\text{nm}$. $N_A = 6.023\times10^{23}\,\text{atoms/mol}$.
Step 1
FCC structure → $n = 4$ atoms per unit cell.
Step 2
Convert atomic radius to cm: $$r = 0.128\;\text{nm} = 0.128\times10^{-7}\;\text{cm} = 1.28\times10^{-8}\;\text{cm}$$
Step 3
FCC lattice parameter (atoms touch along face diagonal, $4r = a\sqrt{2}$): $$a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r = 2\times1.4142\times1.28\times10^{-8} = \boxed{3.620\times10^{-8}\;\text{cm}}$$
Step 4
$$V_c = a^3 = (3.620\times10^{-8})^3 = 47.48\times10^{-24} = \boxed{4.748\times10^{-23}\;\text{cm}^3}$$
Step 5
$$\rho = \frac{n\,A}{N_A\,V_c} = \frac{4\times63.5}{6.023\times10^{23}\times4.748\times10^{-23}} = \frac{254}{28.60} = \boxed{8.88\;\text{g/cm}^3}$$ Actual density of Cu = 8.96 g/cm³ — excellent agreement.

Worked example — moisture content and FSP (every year)
2021/22 · 2022/23 · 2023/24 · 2024/255 marks
Define moisture content and the fibre saturation point. Discuss the effect of changes in moisture content on compressive strength, tensile strength, and elastic modulus of timber.
Moisture content
Def
$$m/c\;(\%) = \frac{w_m - w_0}{w_0}\times100\%$$ where $w_0$ is the mass after oven-drying at 105°C until constant weight. Fresh (green) timber can have $m/c = 40\text{–}200\%$ depending on species.
Fibre Saturation Point
FSP
The FSP is the moisture content at which the cell walls are fully saturated with bound water, but there is no free water in the cell cavities (lumens). It occurs at approximately $m/c \approx 25\%$ for all timber species. It is a critical threshold because mechanical properties only change when $m/c$ falls below the FSP. Above the FSP, removing free water from the cavities has no effect on strength or stiffness.
Effects below FSP
Comp.
Compressive strength parallel to grain increases by approximately 5% for every 1% decrease in $m/c$ below the FSP — the most moisture-sensitive property.
Tensile
Tensile strength perpendicular to grain increases by approximately 1% per 1% decrease in $m/c$ below FSP.
$E$
Elastic modulus increases by approximately 0.2% per 1% decrease in $m/c$ — a modest but real improvement in stiffness.
Trade-off
Work to fracture decreases by ~1% per 1% decrease in $m/c$. Dry timber is stronger and stiffer but more brittle — it absorbs less energy before fracture.
Worked example — why season timber? (2024/25 Q4d)
2024/25 Q4d · 2018/19 Q6c6 marks
Explain why most timber must be seasoned before use for structural purposes and describe the two commonly used seasoning methods.
Why season?
1
Prevent shrinkage and distortion in service. Green timber at $m/c = 40\text{–}100\%$ will dry to equilibrate with its environment (~12–18% in the UK). If installed wet, drying causes shrinkage, warping, and joint failures after installation.
2
Increase strength and stiffness. Below the FSP, each 1% decrease in $m/c$ increases compressive strength by ~5%. Seasoned timber is significantly stronger than green timber.
3
Reduce fungal decay risk. Fungi require moisture $> 20\%$ to survive. Dry, seasoned timber (below 20%) is far more resistant to rot.
4
Reduce weight for easier and cheaper transport and handling.
Method 1 — Air seasoning
Air
Timber is stacked in open sheds with spacers (stickers) between boards to allow air circulation, protected from direct rain and sun. Low cost, no energy. Very slow — months to years. Cannot reliably achieve $m/c < 15\%$ in the UK.
Method 2 — Kiln seasoning
Kiln
Timber is placed in enclosed kilns with controlled temperature and humidity, progressively reduced in stages to drive out moisture without causing stress. Fast (days to weeks). Achieves any required final $m/c$. More expensive due to energy and equipment costs.
Worked example — manufactured timber products (every year)
2021/22 · 2022/23 · 2023/24 · 2024/255 marks
Describe how the disadvantages of natural timber can be overcome by manufactured timber products.
Intro
Natural timber is anisotropic, size-limited, variable due to knots and defects, and susceptible to moisture-induced movement. Manufactured products overcome these by breaking timber down into smaller pieces and reforming them in controlled ways.
Plywood
Thin veneers glued with alternating grain directions. Overcomes: anisotropy (alternating grains give more uniform in-plane properties), dimensional instability (alternating layers restrain each other), and size limitation (wider panels than any single tree).
Chipboard
Wood chips mixed with resin, pressed at ~200°C. Overcomes: uses waste wood and offcuts. Isotropic in-plane. Weaker than plywood, susceptible to creep under sustained load, poor moisture resistance.
MDF
Wood fibres formed into a mat, hot-pressed. Higher pressure → higher density. Softboard → MDF → Hardboard. Overcomes: anisotropy and defects. Smooth surface for painting. Uniform properties throughout.
Glulam
Sections of solid timber finger-jointed and glued in layers. Overcomes: size limitation (can be made to any length or curvature), defect randomisation (knots distributed throughout rather than at one cross-section), and variable quality. Used for large-span structural arches and beams.

Worked example — edge dislocation and plastic deformation (2024/25 Q4b)
2024/25 Q4b · 2023/24 Q4b · 2018/19 Q5c4–5 marks
Explain what is meant by an edge dislocation using a diagram, and discuss its importance in plastic deformation.
Def
An edge dislocation is a line defect consisting of an extra half-plane of atoms inserted into an otherwise perfect crystal lattice. Its end — the dislocation line — runs into the crystal. It is symbolised by ⊥ in 2D cross-section, where the vertical stroke is the extra half-plane and the horizontal stroke is the slip plane.
Why
Without dislocations, plastic deformation would require all atomic bonds across an entire plane to break simultaneously. The theoretical shear stress needed is $\tau_\text{th} \approx G/2\pi \approx 10\text{–}30\;\text{GPa}$ — 1000× higher than what metals actually yield at.
How
Instead, the dislocation glides along the slip plane by breaking and reforming only one bond at a time. As it sweeps across the crystal, the material above the slip plane shifts by one atomic spacing relative to the material below — a permanent plastic deformation, achieved at very low applied stress.
Block
Anything that impedes dislocation motion raises the stress required for plastic flow (the yield strength): grain boundaries, solute atoms (alloying), and precipitates all act as obstacles. This is the basis of all metal strengthening methods.
Worked example — strengthening metals (2024/25 Q4b)
2024/25 Q4b · 2022/23 Q4b6 marks
Describe the two commonly used approaches for strengthening metals. Explain the mechanism in each case and the effect on ductility.
Method 1 — Grain refinement
What
Reducing the average grain size by controlling solidification rate or by cold working.
How
Smaller grains → greater total grain boundary area per unit volume. When a dislocation reaches a grain boundary it cannot continue because the crystal orientation changes. More boundaries (finer grains) means dislocations are blocked more frequently, requiring higher stress to continue plastic flow.
Effect
Yield strength increases. Ductility decreases slightly.
Method 2 — Solid solution alloying
What
Adding atoms of a different element. Substitutional: solute atoms replace host atoms (similar size, e.g. Cu in Ni). Interstitial: small atoms squeeze between host atoms (e.g. C in Fe to make steel).
How
Foreign atoms cause local lattice distortions that create stress fields around them. Moving dislocations must overcome these stress fields, requiring higher applied stress. The yield strength increases.
Effect
Strength increases. Ductility decreases — more solute = more obstacles = harder to deform plastically.

Basics

Fluid basics — pressure, density, viscosity

Everything else builds on these definitions. A fluid continuously deforms under shear stress — this is what separates it from a solid.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Density$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$kg/m³; water ≈ 1000, air ≈ 1.2
Pressure$$P = \frac{F_n}{A}$$Pa = N/m²; normal force per area
Shear stress$$\tau = \frac{F_\tau}{A}$$Pa; tangential force per area
Newton's viscosity law$$\tau = \mu\,\frac{du}{dy}$$$\mu$ = dynamic viscosity (Pa·s)
Kinematic viscosity$$\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$m²/s; water ≈ $10^{-6}$
Mach number$$M = \frac{v}{c}$$$M \ll 1$ → incompressible assumption valid
Knudsen number$$Kn = \frac{\lambda}{L} \ll 1$$Justifies treating fluid as continuum

Solid vs fluid under shear
Solid: applies a shear force → deforms by a fixed amount → stops. The deformation depends on the force.
Fluid: applies a shear force → deforms continuously and never stops. The rate of deformation depends on the force: $\tau = \mu\,du/dy$
Key exam trap: fluids don't have a fixed deformation — they have a fixed rate of deformation under a given shear stress.

Worked example — Newton's viscosity law (2018/19 Q1a)
2018/19 · 2022/23 Q1a type5 marks
A gap of 1 mm between two parallel plates is filled with fluid of viscosity $\mu = 0.2\,\text{Pa·s}$. The upper plate moves at 1 m/s; the lower plate is stationary. The upper plate area is 10 cm². Find the shear force resisting motion.
Setup
The velocity varies linearly from 0 at the bottom to $u = 1$ m/s at the top over $y = 1$ mm = 0.001 m. This gives a constant velocity gradient (Couette flow).
Step 1
Velocity gradient: $$\frac{du}{dy} = \frac{u_\text{top} - u_\text{bot}}{h} = \frac{1 - 0}{0.001} = 1000\;\text{s}^{-1}$$
Step 2
Shear stress by Newton's law: $$\tau = \mu\,\frac{du}{dy} = 0.2 \times 1000 = \boxed{200\;\text{Pa}}$$
Step 3
Convert area: $A = 10\;\text{cm}^2 = 10 \times 10^{-4} = 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2$
Step 4
Shear force: $$F = \tau \times A = 200 \times 10^{-3} = \boxed{0.2\;\text{N}}$$

Hydrostatics

Fluid at rest. Pressure increases with depth. Forces on submerged surfaces. Buoyancy and manometers.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Hydrostatic gradient$$\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$$$z$ upward; pressure rises going down
Pressure at depth $h$$$P = \rho g h$$$h$ = depth below free surface; gauge pressure
Gauge vs absolute$$P_\text{gauge} = P_\text{abs} - P_\text{atm}$$Gauge ignores atmosphere
Pressure head$$h = \frac{P}{\rho g}$$Equivalent height of fluid column
Buoyancy force$$B = \rho_f g V_\text{disp}$$Equals weight of displaced fluid
Float condition$$B = W \;\Rightarrow\; \rho_f V_\text{sub} = m$$Body floats when buoyancy = weight
Uniform load on wall$$F = PL,\quad x_F = L/2$$Rectangular pressure diagram

Inclined manometer$$P - P_a = \rho g\, l\sin\alpha$$$l$ = length along tube, $\alpha$ = inclination
Differential manometer$$\Delta P = (\rho_2 - \rho_1)g h$$Two different fluids

Worked example — force on submerged wall (every year type)
2018/19 Q1b · 2021/22 Q2b type8–12 marks
A vertical rectangular dam wall is 10 m high and 1 m wide. Water ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³) fills to the top. Find the total horizontal force on the wall and the height of the line of action above the base.
Pressure diagram setup
Setup
Pressure varies from $P = 0$ at the top (free surface) to $P = \rho g H = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 10 = 98\,100\;\text{Pa}$ at the base. This is a triangular load.
Total force (area under the triangular pressure diagram)
Force
$$F = \frac{1}{2}\,P_\text{base}\,H\,b = \frac{1}{2} \times 98\,100 \times 10 \times 1 = \boxed{490\,500\;\text{N} = 490.5\;\text{kN}}$$
Location of force (centroid of triangle)
Location
For a triangular load, the resultant acts at $H/3$ from the deep end (base): $$x_F = \frac{H}{3} = \frac{10}{3} = \boxed{3.33\;\text{m above base}}$$
Why?
The centroid of a triangle is at $1/3$ of the height measured from the largest side (the base). Most of the force is near the bottom where pressure is highest, so the resultant is closer to the base than the midpoint.
Worked example — buoyancy (2022/23 Q1a)
2022/23 Q1a type5 marks
A body of volume 1 m³ and mass 2000 kg is fully submerged in (i) water ($\rho_w = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3$) and (ii) oil of relative density 0.9. Find the apparent weight in each fluid.
Weight
$W = mg = 2000 \times 9.81 = 19\,620\;\text{N}$
In water
$$B_w = \rho_w g V = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 1 = 9\,810\;\text{N}$$ $$W_\text{app} = W - B_w = 19\,620 - 9\,810 = \boxed{9\,810\;\text{N}}$$ (object sinks since $W > B$)
In oil
$\rho_\text{oil} = 0.9 \times 1000 = 900\;\text{kg/m}^3$ $$B_\text{oil} = 900 \times 9.81 \times 1 = 8\,829\;\text{N}$$ $$W_\text{app} = 19\,620 - 8\,829 = \boxed{10\,791\;\text{N}}$$ (heavier apparent weight in less dense fluid)

Fluid motion — continuity & acceleration

Streamlines, the continuity equation, and how fluid particles accelerate. Foundation for Bernoulli.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Volumetric flow rate$$Q = A U$$$U$ = mean velocity; $A$ = cross-section area
Mass flow rate$$\dot{m} = \rho Q = \rho A U$$kg/s
Continuity (general)$$\rho_1 A_1 U_1 = \rho_2 A_2 U_2$$Mass conservation
Continuity (incompressible)$$A_1 U_1 = A_2 U_2$$$Q$ = constant along streamtube
Fluid particle acceleration$$a = \frac{\partial U}{\partial t} + U\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}$$Temporal + convective parts
Curved streamline$$\frac{dP}{dr} = \frac{\rho V^2}{r}$$Centripetal force from pressure gradient

Worked example — continuity + acceleration
Tutorial / exam type5 marks
Water flows through a pipe that tapers from diameter $d_1 = 200\;\text{mm}$ to $d_2 = 100\;\text{mm}$. The inlet velocity is $U_1 = 2\;\text{m/s}$ and is steady. Find $U_2$ and the convective acceleration at section 2 if the taper length is 0.5 m.
Areas
$$A_1 = \frac{\pi(0.2)^2}{4} = 0.03142\;\text{m}^2, \quad A_2 = \frac{\pi(0.1)^2}{4} = 0.007854\;\text{m}^2$$
$U_2$
From continuity $A_1 U_1 = A_2 U_2$: $$U_2 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}U_1 = \frac{0.03142}{0.007854}\times 2 = 4\times 2 = \boxed{8\;\text{m/s}}$$
Accel.
Steady flow → $\partial U/\partial t = 0$. Convective acceleration at section 2: $$a = U\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \approx U_2\frac{U_2 - U_1}{L} = 8\times\frac{8-2}{0.5} = \boxed{96\;\text{m/s}^2}$$
Bernoulli

Bernoulli's equation

Energy conservation along a streamline. The single most-used equation in fluid mechanics — and the most mis-applied.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Bernoulli (pressure form)$$P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U^2 + \rho g Z = \text{const}$$Valid along one streamline
Between two points$$P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_1^2 + \rho g Z_1 = P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_2^2 + \rho g Z_2$$Points 1 and 2 on same streamline
Bernoulli (head form)$$\frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{U^2}{2g} + Z = H = \text{const}$$All terms in metres
Stagnation pressure$$P_0 = P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U^2$$All kinetic energy converted to pressure
Reynolds number$$Re = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} = \frac{UL}{\nu}$$Large $Re$ → Bernoulli may apply away from walls
Pressure coefficient$$C_p = \frac{P - P_\infty}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}$$$C_p = 1$ at stagnation point
Bernoulli's four assumptions — must state these in exams: (1) Steady flow (2) Inviscid (no friction) (3) Incompressible (4) Along a single streamline

Worked example — Bernoulli between two points (every year)
Core exam type — every year8–10 marks
A pipe narrows from $d_1 = 100\;\text{mm}$ (section 1) to $d_2 = 50\;\text{mm}$ (section 2). Both sections are horizontal. The pressure at section 1 is $P_1 = 200\;\text{kPa}$ gauge and the flow rate is $Q = 0.015\;\text{m}^3\text{/s}$. Find the pressure at section 2. ($\rho = 1000\;\text{kg/m}^3$)
Areas
$$A_1 = \frac{\pi(0.1)^2}{4} = 7.854\times10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2,\quad A_2 = \frac{\pi(0.05)^2}{4} = 1.963\times10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2$$
Velocities
$$U_1 = \frac{Q}{A_1} = \frac{0.015}{7.854\times10^{-3}} = 1.910\;\text{m/s},\quad U_2 = \frac{Q}{A_2} = \frac{0.015}{1.963\times10^{-3}} = 7.639\;\text{m/s}$$
Bernoulli
Horizontal pipe → $Z_1 = Z_2$, so elevation terms cancel: $$P_2 = P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(U_1^2 - U_2^2) = 200\,000 + \tfrac{1}{2}\times1000\times(1.910^2 - 7.639^2)$$ $$P_2 = 200\,000 + 500\times(3.648 - 58.35) = 200\,000 - 27\,350 = \boxed{172\,650\;\text{Pa} \approx 172.7\;\text{kPa}}$$
Check
$P_2 < P_1$ ✓ — pressure must drop where velocity increases (energy conservation).
Applications

Bernoulli applications — Pitot, Venturi, orifice, weirs

Practical flow measurement devices, all derived from Bernoulli + continuity.

Key formulas
DeviceFormulaNote
Pitot tube velocity$$U = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P)}{\rho}}$$$P_0$ = stagnation, $P$ = static pressure
Venturi (ideal)$$Q_\text{ideal} = A_1 A_2\sqrt{\frac{2(P_1-P_2)}{\rho(A_1^2-A_2^2)}}$$$A_1$ = large section, $A_2$ = throat
Venturi (real)$$Q = C_d\,Q_\text{ideal}$$$C_d$ accounts for viscous losses
Sharp orifice discharge$$Q = C_d\,\frac{\pi d^2}{4}\sqrt{2gh}$$$h$ = head above orifice centre
Discharge coefficients$$C_d = C_v \cdot C_c$$$C_v$ = velocity coeff., $C_c$ = contraction coeff.; typical $C_d \approx 0.6$
Tank emptying time$$T = \frac{1}{C_d}\frac{D^2}{d^2}\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}$$$D$ = tank diam., $d$ = orifice diam.
Rectangular weir$$Q = C_d\,\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{2g}\,b\,h^{3/2}$$$b$ = width, $h$ = head above crest
V-notch weir$$Q = C_d\,\tfrac{8}{15}\tan\alpha\sqrt{2g}\,h^{5/2}$$$\alpha$ = half-angle; very sensitive to $h$

Worked examples
Pitot tube — 2023/24 Q1e type5 marks
A Pitot-static tube is placed in an airflow ($\rho = 1.3\;\text{kg/m}^3$). A connected manometer shows a pressure difference of 20 mm of water. Find the air velocity.
Convert
Pressure difference in Pa: $$\Delta P = \rho_w g h = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.020 = 196.2\;\text{Pa}$$
Velocity
$$U = \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta P}{\rho_\text{air}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\times196.2}{1.3}} = \sqrt{302.0} = \boxed{17.4\;\text{m/s}}$$
Key step
The manometer reads a water height, but we substitute into the Pitot formula using the fluid density of air — not water — because we want the air speed.
Rectangular weir — 2023/24 Q2b/c type8 marks
A rectangular sharp-edged weir is 10 m wide and the head above the crest is $h = 0.12\;\text{m}$. $C_d = 0.60$, $g = 9.81\;\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate $Q$. Does $C_d$ depend on water level?
Formula
$$Q = C_d\,\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{2g}\,b\,h^{3/2} = 0.60\times\frac{2}{3}\times\sqrt{2\times9.81}\times10\times(0.12)^{3/2}$$
Compute
$\sqrt{2\times9.81} = 4.429$; $(0.12)^{3/2} = 0.12\times\sqrt{0.12} = 0.12\times0.3464 = 0.04157$ $$Q = 0.60\times\frac{2}{3}\times4.429\times10\times0.04157 = 0.40\times4.429\times0.4157 = \boxed{0.737\;\text{m}^3\text{/s}}$$
$C_d$?
$C_d$ depends on the ratio $h/b$ — as water level changes, $h/b$ changes, so strictly $C_d$ is not constant. In practice for wide weirs at moderate $h$, the variation is small and $C_d \approx 0.60$ is used.
Pipes & SFEE

Pipes — SFEE, Darcy, Reynolds, losses

Real pipe flow with friction. The SFEE extends Bernoulli to include head losses and pump/turbine work.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Total head$$H = \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{U^2}{2g} + z$$Pressure + velocity + elevation heads
SFEE$$H_1 + \Delta H = H_2 + h_f$$$\Delta H$ = pump head; $h_f$ = total head loss
Horizontal pipe loss$$P_1 - P_2 = \rho g\,h_f$$Pressure drop = friction head loss
Reynolds number (pipe)$$Re = \frac{\rho U d}{\mu} = \frac{Ud}{\nu}$$$Re < 2000$: laminar; $> 4000$: turbulent
Friction factor (laminar)$$f = \frac{16}{Re}$$Only valid for $Re < 2000$
Darcy equation$$h_f = 4f\,\frac{L}{d}\,\frac{U^2}{2g}$$This course uses $4f$ convention
Loss coefficient$$h_i = K_i\,\frac{U^2}{2g}$$For bends, valves, contractions etc.
Pipe friction coefficient$$K_f = \frac{4fL}{d}$$$h_f = K_f U^2/(2g)$
Poiseuille (laminar)$$Q = \frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8\mu L} = \frac{\pi d^4\,\Delta P}{128\mu L}$$$Q \propto \Delta P$ for laminar flow
Series pipes$$h_{f,\text{total}} = \sum h_i = Q^2\sum \kappa_i$$Same $Q$, losses add
Parallel pipes$$Q = \sum Q_i,\quad \kappa_i Q_i^2 = h_f$$Same head loss, flows add
Wall shear stress balance$$\frac{\pi d^2}{4}(P_1-P_2) = \pi d L\,\tau_w$$Pressure force = wall friction force

Worked example — pipe SFEE with Darcy losses (every year)
2018/19 Q1f · 2022/23 Q2b type10–16 marks
Two large open reservoirs are connected by a horizontal pipe 100 m long, 10 cm diameter. The water level in reservoir A is 2 m higher than in B. Friction factor $f = 0.005$. Neglect entry/exit losses. Find the flow velocity and flow rate.
SFEE setup: reservoir A (point 1) to reservoir B (point 2)
Simplify
Large open reservoirs: $P_1 = P_2 = 0$ (gauge), $U_1 \approx U_2 \approx 0$. SFEE reduces to: $$z_1 - z_2 = h_f \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2\;\text{m} = h_f$$
Darcy
$$h_f = 4f\,\frac{L}{d}\,\frac{U^2}{2g} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2 = 4\times0.005\times\frac{100}{0.1}\times\frac{U^2}{2\times9.81}$$
Solve
$$2 = 4\times0.005\times1000\times\frac{U^2}{19.62} = 20\times\frac{U^2}{19.62} = 1.020\,U^2$$ $$U^2 = \frac{2}{1.020} = 1.961 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{U = 1.40\;\text{m/s}}$$
Flow rate
$$Q = A\,U = \frac{\pi(0.1)^2}{4}\times1.40 = 7.854\times10^{-3}\times1.40 = \boxed{1.10\times10^{-2}\;\text{m}^3\text{/s}}$$
Check Re
$$Re = \frac{Ud}{\nu} = \frac{1.40\times0.1}{10^{-6}} = 140\,000 \gg 4000\quad\checkmark\text{ turbulent, Moody needed for precise }f$$
Worked example — laminar pipe flow + Reynolds number
2018/19 Q1e · 2024/25 Q1e type5–6 marks
Water ($\mu = 10^{-3}\;\text{Pa·s}$) flows through a pipe of diameter 1 cm at a flow rate of 1 litre/min. Is the flow laminar or turbulent? If laminar, find $f$.
Convert
$Q = 1\;\text{litre/min} = \frac{1\times10^{-3}}{60} = 1.667\times10^{-5}\;\text{m}^3\text{/s}$
Velocity
$$U = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{1.667\times10^{-5}}{\pi(0.01)^2/4} = \frac{1.667\times10^{-5}}{7.854\times10^{-5}} = 0.212\;\text{m/s}$$
Reynolds
$$Re = \frac{\rho U d}{\mu} = \frac{1000\times0.212\times0.01}{10^{-3}} = \boxed{2120}$$
Regime
$Re = 2120$ is in the transition zone (2000–4000). Nominally close to laminar; if strictly laminar: $$f = \frac{16}{Re} = \frac{16}{2120} = \boxed{0.00755}$$
Pumps & systems

Pumps, operating points & parallel/series pipes

How pumps interact with pipe systems. Series and parallel configurations. The operating point is where the pump curve meets the system curve.

Key formulas

Pump water power$$P_w = \rho g Q\,\Delta H$$Useful power delivered to water

Operating point$$\Delta H_\text{pump}(Q) = \Delta H_\text{sys}(Q)$$Intersection of pump and system curves
Three-reservoir junction$$z_1 - H_0 = \kappa_1 Q_1^2,\quad Q_1 = Q_2 + Q_3$$Guess $H_0$, iterate for continuity

Worked example — pump power and efficiency
2021/22 Q1b type8 marks
A pump delivers water at $Q = 0.037\;\text{m}^3\text{/s}$ through two parallel pipes each 45 m long, 100 mm diameter, $f = 0.008$. One pipe discharges to a reservoir 6 m above the pump; the other to a reservoir 8 m above. The pump inlet is 0.6 m below the lower reservoir. Pump efficiency $\eta = 0.6$. Find the shaft power.
Setup
Two parallel pipes from the same pump outlet. Each pipe has the same $d$, $L$, $f$. They share the total head at the pump outlet but lead to different reservoir levels.
Each pipe $\kappa$
$$\kappa_i = \frac{4fL}{d}\frac{1}{2gA^2} = \frac{4\times0.008\times45}{0.1}\times\frac{1}{2\times9.81\times(7.854\times10^{-3})^2} = 14.4\times\frac{1}{1.208\times10^{-3}} = \frac{14.4}{1.208\times10^{-3}}$$
Parallel
Both pipes have same $\kappa$. Parallel condition: $\kappa Q_1^2 = \Delta H - 6$ and $\kappa Q_2^2 = \Delta H - 8$ and $Q_1 + Q_2 = 0.037$. With pump head $\Delta H$ and numerical $\kappa$, solve iteratively or use the SFEE approach as shown in the full tutorial solution.
Power
Once $\Delta H$ is found: $$P_w = \rho g Q\,\Delta H,\quad P_s = \frac{P_w}{\eta}$$
