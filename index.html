Calculation

Stress, strain & elastic constants

The core bar problem. Appears in every single exam. Master this first.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaUnits / note
Cross-section area (circle)A = πd²/4mm² if d in mm
Normal stressσ = F / AMPa = N/mm²
Axial strainε = ΔL / Ldimensionless
Young's modulusE = σ / εGPa or MPa
Poisson's ratioν = −ε_lat / ε_axialtypically 0.2–0.35
Diameter changeΔd = −ν(ΔL/L)dnegative = decreases in tension
E–G–ν relationshipE = 2G(1 + ν)rearrange for ν or G
Secant modulusE_sec = σ / ε at chosen pointNOT the initial slope
Elastic strain energyU = ½σε (per unit vol)area under linear part
Step-by-step method
1
Write down all given values. Convert: kN → N, nm → mm (×10⁻⁶)
2
Calculate area: A = πd²/4
3
Stress: σ = F/A → units are MPa if N and mm²
4
Strain: ε = ΔL / L (dimensionless)
5
Modulus: E = σ/ε in MPa → divide by 1000 for GPa
6
If G is given: ν = E/(2G) − 1
7
Diameter change: Δd = −ν × (ΔL/L) × d
Common traps
!
Using d directly — always compute A = πd²/4 first
!
Unit mismatch: 20 kN ÷ mm² → don't mix. Use 20,000 N ÷ mm² for MPa
!
Secant modulus ≠ Young's modulus. It's σ/ε at a specific point on the curve
!
Proof stress offset: 0.1% = ε = 0.001, not 0.1. Draw parallel line starting at ε = 0.001
!
Δd is negative (bar gets thinner in tension). Don't drop the minus sign
Worked examples
2024/25 · 2022/23 · 2021/22 7 marks
A metal bar is 175 mm long, diameter 14 mm. A 20 kN tensile force produces an extension of 0.14 mm. The shear modulus G = 65 GPa.

(i) Calculate the modulus of elasticity.
(ii) Calculate Poisson's ratio and the change in diameter of the bar.
i
Area: A = π(14)²/4 = 153.94 mm²
Stress: σ = 20,000 / 153.94 = 129.9 MPa
Strain: ε = 0.14 / 175 = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴
E = 129.9 / (8.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 162,375 MPa = 162.4 GPa
ii
ν = E/(2G) − 1 = 162.4/(2 × 65) − 1 = 1.249 − 1 = 0.249
Δd = −ν × (ΔL/L) × d = −0.249 × (0.14/175) × 14 = −2.8 × 10⁻³ mm (bar gets thinner)
2022/23 Q3b 11 marks
From a load-extension table for a 150 mm long, 20 mm diameter bar, determine:

(i) The 0.1% proof stress
(ii) The secant modulus at 60% of the 0.1% proof stress
(iii) The total work to failure (bar fails at load 75 kN, extension 0.66 mm)
(iv) Poisson's ratio and shear modulus if diameter reduced by 0.021 mm at 30 kN
i
A = π(20)²/4 = 314.16 mm². Plot σ vs ε from load-extension table. Draw a line parallel to the initial elastic slope, starting at ε = 0.001 (= 0.1%). Where it intersects the curve = proof stress. Typically ~195–205 MPa for this dataset.
ii
Find the point at 60% of proof stress. Secant modulus = σ at that point ÷ ε at that point. E_sec = σ/ε at that specific point — not the initial slope.
iii
Work to failure = area under load-extension curve. Use trapezium rule: W = Σ[(F₁+F₂)/2 × (e₂−e₁)]. Convert to Joules: 1 kN·mm = 1 J. Sum all trapezoids from 0 to failure.
iv
At 30 kN: σ = 30,000/314.16 = 95.5 MPa, ε_axial = 0.105/150 = 7×10⁻⁴
ε_lateral = Δd/d = −0.021/20 = −1.05×10⁻³
ν = −ε_lat / ε_axial = 1.05×10⁻³ / 7×10⁻⁴ = 0.30
E = 95.5 / 7×10⁻⁴ = 136,400 MPa = 136.4 GPa → G = E/[2(1+ν)] = 136.4/[2×1.30] = 52.5 GPa
2023/24 Q3a 5 marks
A metal bar (diameter 15 mm, length 100 mm) is loaded in tension. At a load of 20 kN the extension is 0.100 mm and the shear modulus G = 47.1 GPa.

Calculate Poisson's ratio and the reduction in diameter at 20 kN.
1
A = π(15)²/4 = 176.71 mm²
2
σ = 20,000 / 176.71 = 113.2 MPa
3
ε = 0.100 / 100 = 1.0 × 10⁻³
4
E = 113.2 / 1.0×10⁻³ = 113,200 MPa = 113.2 GPa
5
ν = E/(2G) − 1 = 113.2/(94.2) − 1 = 0.202
6
Δd = −0.202 × (0.100/100) × 15 = −3.03 × 10⁻³ mm
Calculation

Creep & stress relaxation

Appears in every exam. Two numbers calculated: creep coefficient φ and effective modulus E_eff.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Initial elastic strainε_e = σ / ECalculate this first
Creep coefficientφ = ε_creep / ε_eDimensionless
Total strainε_total = ε_e + ε_creepAdd elastic + creep
Effective modulusE_eff = σ / ε_totalUse total strain, not just elastic
Creep rate lawdε/dt = C·σⁿFor finding C and n from two tests
Find nn = log(rate₁/rate₂) / log(σ₁/σ₂)Divide two rate equations to cancel C
Unit conversions — do these before calculating:
1200 microstrain = 1200 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0012
0.25% strain = 0.25/100 = 0.0025
0.1% = 0.001
Step-by-step method
1
Calculate elastic strain: ε_e = σ/E
2
Convert given creep strain to decimal (microstrain × 10⁻⁶, % ÷ 100)
3
Creep coefficient: φ = ε_creep / ε_e
4
Total strain: ε_total = ε_e + ε_creep
5
Effective modulus: E_eff = σ / ε_total
6
Repeat steps 2–5 for each time measurement given
Common traps
!
Entering microstrain directly — always multiply by 10⁻⁶
!
Using E_eff = σ/ε_creep — wrong. Must use ε_total = ε_e + ε_creep
!
Forgetting to calculate ε_e first before doing φ
!
Creep vs stress relaxation: creep = constant stress, strain grows. Relaxation = constant strain, stress drops
Worked examples
2024/25 · 2023/24 · 2021/22 6 marks
A material with elastic modulus E = 95 GPa is loaded to a stress of 175 MPa. Creep strains are measured at 3 days and 6 months and found to be 1200 microstrain and 0.25% respectively.

Calculate the creep coefficient and effective modulus at each measurement time.
1
Elastic strain: ε_e = σ/E = 175 MPa / 95,000 MPa = 1.842 × 10⁻³
——
— At 3 days —
2
Convert: 1200 microstrain = 1200 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.2 × 10⁻³
3
φ = ε_creep / ε_e = 1.2×10⁻³ / 1.842×10⁻³ = 0.651
4
ε_total = 1.842×10⁻³ + 1.2×10⁻³ = 3.042×10⁻³
5
E_eff = 175 / 3.042×10⁻³ = 57,530 MPa = 57.5 GPa
——
— At 6 months —
6
Convert: 0.25% = 0.0025 = 2.5 × 10⁻³
7
φ = 2.5×10⁻³ / 1.842×10⁻³ = 1.357
8
ε_total = 1.842×10⁻³ + 2.5×10⁻³ = 4.342×10⁻³
9
E_eff = 175 / 4.342×10⁻³ = 40,300 MPa = 40.3 GPa
Creep rate law (tutorial type) C & n
Two creep tests give: Test 1: σ = 100 MPa, creep rate = 2×10⁻⁸/s. Test 2: σ = 200 MPa, creep rate = 3.2×10⁻⁷/s.

Find n and C. Hence find time to reach 0.1% creep strain at σ = 150 MPa.
1
n = log(rate₁/rate₂) / log(σ₁/σ₂) = log(2×10⁻⁸ / 3.2×10⁻⁷) / log(100/200)
n = log(0.0625) / log(0.5) = (−1.204) / (−0.301) = 4.0
2
C = rate / σⁿ = 2×10⁻⁸ / (100)⁴ = 2×10⁻¹⁶
3
Rate at 150 MPa: dε/dt = 2×10⁻¹⁶ × (150)⁴ = 2×10⁻¹⁶ × 5.0625×10⁸ = 1.013×10⁻⁷/s
4
Target: 0.1% = 0.001. Time = ε / rate = 0.001 / 1.013×10⁻⁷ = 9,870 s ≈ 2.74 hours
Calculation

Fatigue & Miner's rule

Failure under repeated loading. Key skill: reading S-N curves and applying Miner's rule for cumulative damage.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Stress rangeS = σ_max − σ_minIf σ_min is negative, subtracting it increases S
Mean stressσ_mean = (σ_max + σ_min) / 2—
Miner's ruleΣ(nᵢ / Nᵢ) = 1Failure when sum reaches 1
Time to failuret = N / ff = frequency in Hz
Time using periodt = N × TT = period of one cycle
S-N curve method
1
Calculate stress range S = σ_max − σ_min
2
Read N from S-N curve at that stress range
3
For Miner's rule: damage = n/N per loading block
4
Sum until Σ(n/N) = 1 → failure
5
Remaining life = N_remaining at that stress
Common traps
!
σ_min = −50 MPa: S = 100 − (−50) = 150 MPa, NOT 50 MPa
!
Frequency doesn't change N (cycles to failure) — only changes time to failure
!
Endurance limit: if S is below this, the material never fails by fatigue
!
Corrosion (e.g. seawater) eliminates the endurance limit — failure will eventually occur
Worked example
Miner's rule type 6 marks
A component is subjected to: 2×10⁵ cycles at S = 400 MPa (N = 5×10⁵), then 1×10⁶ cycles at S = 200 MPa (N = 8×10⁶).
How many additional cycles at S = 300 MPa (N = 2×10⁶) will cause failure?
1
Damage from block 1: n/N = 2×10⁵ / 5×10⁵ = 0.40
2
Damage from block 2: n/N = 1×10⁶ / 8×10⁶ = 0.125
3
Damage so far: 0.40 + 0.125 = 0.525
4
Remaining damage capacity: 1 − 0.525 = 0.475
5
Remaining cycles at S = 300 MPa: n = 0.475 × 2×10⁶ = 9.5 × 10⁵ cycles
Calc + Theory

Variability & characteristic strength

How we account for the fact that no two samples have exactly the same strength. Used in every structural design code.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Mean strengthσ_m = Σσ / nSum all values, divide by count
Standard deviations = √[Σ(σ − σ_m)² / n]Measure of spread
Characteristic strengthσ_k = σ_m − k·sk depends on failure rate
Timber characteristicσ_k = σ_m − 1.64·s5% failure rate standard
Design stressσ_d = σ_k / γ_mγ_m = material partial factor
Design loadF_d = F_k × γ_fγ_f = load partial factor
k values for failure rate: 10% → k = 1.28  |  5% (1 in 20) → k = 1.64  |  2% (1 in 50) → k = 1.96  |  1% → k = 2.33
Worked example
2022/23 Q3c 4 marks
Tests on 10 samples of mild steel gave yield stresses of: 265, 275, 271, 263, 255, 240, 263, 272, 270, 262 MPa.

Calculate the characteristic yield strength based on a 1 in 50 failure rate.
1
Sum = 265+275+271+263+255+240+263+272+270+262 = 2636
2
Mean: σ_m = 2636 / 10 = 263.6 MPa
3
Deviations squared: (265−263.6)²=1.96, (275−263.6)²=129.96, (271−263.6)²=54.76, (263−263.6)²=0.36, (255−263.6)²=73.96, (240−263.6)²=556.96, (263−263.6)²=0.36, (272−263.6)²=70.56, (270−263.6)²=40.96, (262−263.6)²=2.56
4
Σ = 932.4 → s = √(932.4/10) = √93.24 = 9.66 MPa
5
1 in 50 → k = 1.96
6
σ_k = 263.6 − 1.96 × 9.66 = 263.6 − 18.9 = 244.7 MPa

Crystal density (FCC/BCC)

Appears in every single exam. Always FCC. Always the same method. Learn the steps and you will always get this right.

Key formulas
QuantityFormulaNote
Theoretical densityρ = nA / (N_A × V_c)n = atoms/cell, A = atomic weight, N_A = 6.023×10²³
FCC: atoms per celln = 48 corners × ⅛ + 6 faces × ½
FCC: lattice parametera = 2√2 · rAtoms touch along face diagonal
BCC: atoms per celln = 28 corners × ⅛ + 1 centre
BCC: lattice parametera = 4r / √3Atoms touch along body diagonal
Unit cell volumeV_c = a³All cubic structures
Unit warning: If r is in nm, convert to cm: 0.128 nm = 0.128 × 10⁻⁷ cm. Use A in g/mol. Result will be in g/cm³.
FCC method (every exam)
1
n = 4 (FCC always)
2
Convert r: nm → cm (multiply by 10⁻⁷)
3
a = 2√2 · r
4
V_c = a³
5
ρ = (4 × A) / (6.023×10²³ × V_c)
6
Result is in g/cm³ — compare to known value

Worked examples
2024/25 Q4a — Copper 6 marks
Calculate the theoretical density of copper (FCC): atomic weight = 63.5 g/mol, atomic radius = 0.128 nm. Avogadro's number = 6.023 × 10²³.
1
n = 4 (FCC)
2
r = 0.128 nm = 0.128 × 10⁻⁷ cm = 1.28 × 10⁻⁸ cm
3
a = 2√2 × 1.28×10⁻⁸ = 2 × 1.4142 × 1.28×10⁻⁸ = 3.620 × 10⁻⁸ cm
4
V_c = (3.620×10⁻⁸)³ = 4.748 × 10⁻²³ cm³
5
ρ = (4 × 63.5) / (6.023×10²³ × 4.748×10⁻²³) = 254 / 28.59 = 8.89 g/cm³
✓
Actual density of copper ≈ 8.96 g/cm³ — close match confirms method is correct

Calculate the theoretical density of palladium (FCC): A = 106.4 g/mol, r = 0.138 nm.

Theory

Timber

High-frequency theory topic. Questions repeat across years. Learn the key facts and mechanisms, not just definitions.

Moisture content
Formula—
m/c = (w_m − w_0) / w_0 × 100%w_0 = oven-dry weight at 105°C
FSP (Fibre Saturation Point) ≈ 25% m/c
Below FSP: bound water leaves cell walls → shrinkage, strength changes
Above FSP: only free water in cavities → no dimensional change
For every 1% decrease in m/c below FSP:
Compressive strength ↑ ~5%  |  Flexural strength ↑ ~4%
Tensile strength ⊥ ↑ ~1%  |  E ↑ ~0.2%
Work to fracture ↓ ~1% (becomes more brittle)
Shrinkage directions
DirectionShrinkage (green → 12%)
Longitudinal< 0.1% (negligible)
Radial1.5 – 4.5%
Tangential2 – 9.5% (highest)
Memory shortcut: Tangential > Radial > Longitudinal
Reason: growth rings resist radial shrinkage; no restraint tangentially
Why season timber?
→
Reduce shrinkage and distortion in service — if used wet it will shrink and warp after installation
→
Increase strength and stiffness — dry timber below FSP is significantly stronger
→
Reduce risk of fungal decay — fungi need moisture to survive; dry timber won't rot
→
Match service environment — season to the m/c consistent with where the timber will be used
Air seasoning: Stack protected from rain/sun with air circulation. Slow (months–years). Low cost. May not reach low enough m/c.
Kiln seasoning: Forced drying in kilns with heat and humidity control. Fast (days–weeks). Controllable final m/c. More expensive.
Grain direction & strength
Timber is anisotropic — strength depends on direction. Strength is highest when loading is parallel to grain. As loading angle to grain increases, strength falls significantly. Knots redirect grain locally and create stress concentrations.
FeatureEffect on strength
Loading parallel to grainMaximum strength
Loading perpendicular to grainMuch lower strength (especially in tension)
KnotsRedirect grain, reduce strength and E; effect proportional to knot size
Wane / fissuresReduce effective cross-section and reliability
Manufactured timber products
Purpose: overcome timber's anisotropy, natural size limits, and variability due to defects by breaking it down and reforming it.
ProductMethodKey property
PlywoodThin veneers glued with alternating grain directionsIsotropic in-plane, dimensionally stable
Chipboard (particleboard)Chips + resin, pressed at ~200°CCheap, weaker, prone to creep, needs frequent support
Fibreboard (MDF)Fibres broken down, mat formed, pressed at ~200°CHigher pressure = higher density. Softboard → MDF → Hardboard
GlulamSections glued together in layersLarge structural shapes, defects randomised, less limited by size
Worked examples
2021/22 Q4c + 2022/23 Q4c 5 marks
Define moisture content and the fibre saturation point for timber. Discuss the effect of changes in moisture content on the compressive strength, tensile strength, and elastic modulus of timber.
1
Moisture content: m/c = (w_m − w_0)/w_0 × 100. w_0 is the oven-dry weight at 105°C. Freshly cut timber can have m/c of 40–200%.
2
FSP: The m/c at which cell walls are saturated with bound water but no free water exists in cell cavities. ≈ 25% for most species.
3
Above FSP: Only free water changes — no effect on mechanical properties. Strength and stiffness remain constant.
4
Below FSP: Each 1% decrease in m/c gives: compressive strength ↑ ~5%, tensile ⊥ ↑ ~1%, flexural ↑ ~4%, E ↑ ~0.2%. Timber becomes stronger and stiffer but more brittle (work to fracture ↓ ~1%).
Theory

Material structure & strengthening

Why metals deform plastically, how dislocations work, and how we make metals stronger.

Dislocations
A dislocation is a line defect in the crystal — an extra half-plane of atoms. When stress is applied, dislocations move (slip), causing permanent plastic deformation. Without dislocations, metals would need 1000× more stress to yield.
→
Edge dislocation: extra half-plane of atoms, Burgers vector ⊥ to dislocation line
→
Movement of dislocations = plastic (permanent) deformation
→
Block dislocation movement → increase yield strength
Grain boundaries
Grains are regions of uniform crystal orientation. Where two grains meet at different orientations = grain boundary.
→
Grain boundaries block dislocation movement
→
Smaller grains → more boundaries → higher yield strength
→
BUT smaller grains also means less ductility (trade-off)
Two strengthening methods
MethodMechanismEffect on ductility
Grain refinementMore grain boundaries block dislocationsDecreases (trade-off)
AlloyingForeign atoms (substitutional or interstitial) distort lattice and hinder dislocation glideDecreases (trade-off)
Substitutional solid solution: alloy atom replaces host atom (similar size). E.g. Cu in Ni.
Interstitial solid solution: small atom fits between host atoms. E.g. carbon in iron (steel).
Polymer strengthening (cross-linking)
Cross-linking adds covalent bonds between polymer chains. This restricts chain movement → higher strength, higher stiffness, lower ductility. More cross-links = stiffer and more brittle. Example: vulcanised rubber vs natural rubber.
Chain typeStrengthDuctility
LinearLowestHighest
BranchedLowHigh
Cross-linkedMedium–highMedium
NetworkHighestLowest (brittle)

Bitumen & asphalt

Understanding viscoelastic behaviour, ageing, and the three main pavement failure types.

Flexible vs rigid pavement
PropertyFlexible (asphalt)Rigid (concrete)
StiffnessLowerHigher
DeflectionBends/deflectsVery little
Load distributionCone — reduces with depthSlab — distributes uniformly
Failure modeRutting, fatigue crackingCracking, joint failures
Bitumen properties
→
Viscosity: resistance to flow. Increases as temperature decreases. More asphaltenes = higher viscosity.
→
Penetration: distance needle penetrates under standard load/time/temperature. Higher pen = softer bitumen.
→
Softening point: temperature at which bitumen is soft enough that a steel ball falls a fixed distance.
→
Viscoelasticity: elastic component (recoverable) + viscous component (permanent). Loading rate and temperature control which dominates.
Ageing mechanism
Bitumen hardens in service over time. Two causes:

1. Oxidation: Oxygen converts aromatics and resins → asphaltenes, increasing viscosity and brittleness.
2. Loss of volatiles: Lighter oily fractions evaporate or oxidise away during heating and service.
Short-term ageing: during heating/mixing in the plant. Long-term ageing: during years of service. Both make bitumen harder and more brittle → increases risk of low-temperature cracking.
Three main pavement failures — memory shortcut
FailureTriggerMechanism
RuttingHot weatherAsphalt softens, loses elasticity → permanent deformation under wheel loads
Fatigue crackingRepeated loading + ageingCrack initiation and growth under cyclic stress; accelerated by harder/aged bitumen
Low-temp crackingCold weatherBitumen becomes brittle; thermal contraction produces tensile stresses → cracks
Shortcut: Hot → Rutting  |  Repeated loads → Fatigue  |  Cold → Brittle cracking
